1.2 Robot Physical Specifications & Dimensional Engineering

The mobile robot (animal_robot) is engineered as a compact, highly agile differential-drive platform designed to maneuver through dense woodland vegetation without snagging on tree trunks or boulders.

Chassis Structure & Inertia

  • Chassis Dimensions: Length 0.22 meters, Width 0.12 meters, Height 0.06 meters.
  • Chassis Net Mass: 3.0 kilograms.
  • Center of Mass (COM): Positioned at [0.0, 0.0, -0.02] meters. Placing the mass center below the wheel axle level provides strong passive pitch and roll self-righting stability during sharp acceleration.
  • Inertia Tensor:
    Ixx = 0.008, Iyy = 0.015, Izz = 0.020 kg·m².

Wheel Actuators & Caster Sphere

  • Drive Wheels: 2 actuated wheels, radius r = 0.035 meters (diameter 0.07 meters), length 0.02 meters, mass 0.3 kilograms each.
  • Track Separation (L): 0.12 meters between left and right wheel midpoints.
  • Wheel Friction: High traction μ1 = 1.5, μ2 = 1.5, stiffness Kp = 100000.0, Kd = 10.0.
  • Passive Support: Front caster sphere, radius 0.015 meters, mass 0.2 kilograms, near-zero friction μ = 0.001 allowing frictionless omnidirectional gliding.

1.3 Differential Drive Kinematics (Human-Readable Formulations)

The robot operates on a 2-dimensional flat ground plane. At any moment, the robot's physical state is defined by its position (X, Y) and its heading angle (θ) pointing toward where it is facing.

1. Forward Speed Formula (v)
Forward Velocity (v) = (Right Wheel Speed + Left Wheel Speed) ÷ 2
In terms of wheel spin: v = 0.5 × Wheel Radius × (ωRight + ωLeft)
Using our robot's wheel radius (r = 0.035 meters): v = 0.0175 × (ωRight + ωLeft) m/s
What this means in plain words: The robot's straight-ahead velocity is simply the average ground speed of the two drive wheels. If both wheels spin forward at the exact same speed, the robot travels straight forward.
2. Turning Yaw Speed Formula (ω)
Turning Rate (ω) = (Right Wheel Speed − Left Wheel Speed) ÷ Wheel Track Distance (L)
In terms of wheel spin: ω = (Wheel Radius / Track Distance) × (ωRight − ωLeft)
Using our robot dimensions (r = 0.035 m, Track L = 0.12 m): ω = 0.2917 × (ωRight − ωLeft) rad/s
What this means in plain words: Turning is created by the speed difference between the two wheels. If the right wheel spins faster than the left wheel, the robot curves to the left. If the wheels spin in opposite directions at equal speed, the robot spins in place around its own center!
3. Inverse Kinematics: How the Computer Commands the Wheel Motors
Right Wheel Motor Speed (ωRight) = (Desired Forward Speed + 0.5 × Track × Desired Turn Speed) ÷ Wheel Radius
Left Wheel Motor Speed (ωLeft) = (Desired Forward Speed − 0.5 × Track × Desired Turn Speed) ÷ Wheel Radius
Plugging in our robot values (r = 0.035 meters, Track L = 0.12 meters):
• Right Motor Speed (ωRight) = (v + 0.06 × ω) ÷ 0.035 rad/s
• Left Motor Speed (ωLeft) = (v − 0.06 × ω) ÷ 0.035 rad/s
What this means in plain words: When the autonomous navigation system decides that the robot should move forward at 0.22 m/s while gently turning at 0.14 rad/s, these two formulas calculate the exact motor speeds to send to each wheel. This runs automatically at 50 Hz inside the Gazebo differential drive plugin.
4. Turning Radius (R) & Instantaneous Center of Curvature
Turning Radius (R) = Forward Speed (v) ÷ Turning Speed (ω)
In terms of wheel spin: R = 0.06 × [(ωRight + ωLeft) / (ωRight − ωLeft)] meters
What this means in plain words:
• Straight line motion: Both wheels spin at the same speed → Turning Radius is infinite (no turn).
• In-place zero-radius spin: Wheels spin in opposite directions (ωRight = −ωLeft) → Turning Radius = 0 (the robot spins on its own center point like a tank, which is used in the 360° environmental scan).
5. Driving Constraint: No Sideways Slipping (Non-Holonomic Motion)
Instantaneous Lateral Sliding Speed = 0.0 m/s
What this means in plain words: Like a regular passenger car, a differential-drive robot cannot slide sideways directly. The wheels grip the ground and only allow motion forward, backward, or in an arc. To reach a spot to its side, the robot must first turn to face that direction and then drive forward.

1.5 Gazebo Differential Drive Plugin Integration

The ODE physics engine in Gazebo does not accept raw velocity commands directly on joints without a controller. The libgazebo_ros_diff_drive.so plugin acts as the digital closed-loop servo amplifier:

  • It binds to joint_right_wheel and joint_left_wheel.
  • It subscribes to /animal_robot/cmd_vel (geometry_msgs/msg/Twist).
  • It applies torque (up to 20.0 N·m) with maximum rotational acceleration limit 5.0 rad/s² to reach target speeds smoothly.
  • It integrates wheel revolutions over time to publish dead-reckoning odometry on /animal_robot/odom and broadcasts the spatial transform from odom to chassis.

1.7 Part 1 Dedicated Viva & Oral Exam Defense Guide

Q1.1 How does the robot turn in place with zero turning radius, and how do the wheel speeds relate?
Topic Focus: Differential Drive Kinematics
Model Answer: To turn in place with a zero turning radius, the robot commands both drive wheels to spin at the exact same rotational speed but in opposite directions (Right Wheel = −Left Wheel).

Since the forward speed is the sum of both wheel speeds divided by two, the net forward speed becomes zero (v = 0 m/s). Meanwhile, the turning speed is proportional to the difference between the wheels, producing maximum rotation around the center point between the wheels. This allows our robot to execute 360-degree environmental scans in dense brush without needing any space to move forward or backward.
Q1.2 Why is the chassis center of mass (COM) set at [0, 0, -0.02] meters instead of [0, 0, 0]?
Topic Focus: Mechanical Stability & Dynamics
Model Answer: Setting the center of mass 2 centimeters below the wheel axle creates a natural pendulum-like self-stabilizing effect. If the center of mass were positioned high above the wheels, rapid acceleration or sudden braking would cause the chassis to pitch and tilt aggressively forward or backward. A low center of mass keeps the camera horizontal and stable, preventing distorted camera frames.
Q1.3 What is the role of robot_state_publisher and how does it interface with URDF?
Topic Focus: Coordinate Systems & TF2 Tree
Model Answer: robot_state_publisher reads the robot description XML (animal_robot.urdf) and listens to the joint positions published on /joint_states. It calculates the 3D position and orientation of every single component of the robot and broadcasts them dynamically as coordinate transforms over the /tf topic. This allows the computer vision and navigation nodes to know the exact 3D position of the camera relative to the chassis wheels.
Q1.4 Why did you use continuous joints for the drive wheels rather than revolute joints?
Topic Focus: URDF Joint Modeling
Model Answer: In URDF, a revolute joint is a rotational joint that has strict physical angle limits (for example, rotating between -90° and +90°, like a robot elbow). A continuous joint has no angle limits and can spin indefinitely without stopping. Because drive wheels must spin continuously to propel the robot forward over long distances, they must be declared as type="continuous".
Q1.5 Explain how wheel friction parameters μ1 and μ2 impact simulation realism.
Topic Focus: Physics & Contact Friction
Model Answer: In the ODE physics engine, μ1 represents friction along the primary direction of rolling, and μ2 represents friction along the perpendicular sideways direction. For the drive wheels, setting μ1 = μ2 = 1.5 models high-traction rubber on soil, preventing wheel slippage. For the front caster, setting μ1 = μ2 = 0.001 creates an omnidirectional, nearly frictionless glide pad that eliminates lateral scrubbing resistance during sharp turns.

2.2 Gazebo Simulation Engine & SDF 1.6 vs URDF Architecture

Gazebo Classic 11 relies on the Open Dynamics Engine (ODE) as its underlying physics solver. While the mobile robot is defined using URDF, the simulated world and all static environment entities (trees, rocks, animals, lighting) are defined using the Simulation Description Format (SDF 1.6):

  • Why SDF for Worlds? URDF is strictly limited to tree-structured kinematic mechanisms with a single root link. It cannot represent multiple independent free-floating models, world physics parameters, ambient lighting, directional sun vectors, or global ground planes. SDF natively models entire multi-body environments.
  • The World File (animal_world.world): Acts as the top-level container, instantiating ODE solver parameters, friction models, sunlight properties, and nested model inclusions using the model:// URI schema.

2.3 Procedural Wildlife World Generator (generate_random_world.py)

A critical limitation of typical student robotics projects is evaluating algorithms in static, manually constructed worlds. If the animal is always placed at the exact same coordinates, the system can easily overfit. The project includes a dedicated procedural generation engine (packages/animal_gazebo/scripts/generate_random_world.py) that synthesizes realistic, organic forest reserves on demand:

Spatial Collision Check Algorithmgenerate_random_world.py
def is_valid_position(self, x, y, radius): # 1. Boundary check within arena walls (22m x 22m) if abs(x) > (self.half_size - 1.0) or abs(y) > (self.half_size - 1.0): return False # 2. Safety radius: keep 2.0m obstacle-free circle around robot spawn (0, 0) if self.distance((x, y), (0.0, 0.0)) < (self.spawn_clearance_radius + radius): return False # 3. Distance check: ensure no overlap with existing placed objects for ox, oy, oradius, _ in self.placed_objects: if self.distance((x, y), (ox, oy)) < (radius + oradius): return False return True
Obstacle Spacing Rule (No Overlapping Objects)
Distance Between Objects ≥ Radius of Object 1 + Radius of Object 2
What this means in plain words: Before placing any tree, boulder, or deer into the simulated forest, the code calculates the distance to every already-placed object. If the distance is smaller than the combined size of both objects, the new object is rejected and re-placed elsewhere. It also guarantees a 2.0-meter empty circle around the robot spawn point so the robot never gets stuck inside a tree.

🌲 Strategic Companion Cover Placement
In real ecosystems, camouflaged prey animals stand immediately adjacent to trees or boulders to disrupt their physical silhouette. In generate_random_world.py, whenever a deer is placed, the algorithm automatically spawns a companion natural cover object (an oak tree, pine tree, or rock boulder) at a randomized distance of 0.6 to 1.2 meters from the deer:
cx = dx + cover_dist * cos(cover_angle) ; cy = dy + cover_dist * sin(cover_angle)
This forces the AI to detect the animal based on micro-texture rather than relying on an isolated silhouette outline against an empty background!

2.5 Solar Lighting Domain Randomization

Lighting in outdoor environments varies by time of day, cloud cover, and solar zenith. The generator applies photometric domain randomization by computing a randomized 3D solar direction vector:

Solar Direction Vector Formula
Sun Direction X = −cos(Compass Angle) × cos(Elevation Angle)
Sun Direction Y = −sin(Compass Angle) × cos(Elevation Angle)
Sun Direction Z = −sin(Elevation Angle)
Where Compass Angle is randomized between 0° and 360° (azimuth), and Sun Elevation is randomized between 35° and 70° above the ground plane.
What this means in plain words: The sunlight angle changes randomly every time a new world is generated. This creates dynamic shadows and realistic lighting (from golden-hour morning sun to bright midday glare), ensuring the AI learns real animal textures rather than memorizing fixed shadow shapes.

⚡ Physics Performance: Visual vs Collision Mesh Decoupling
A major breakthrough in our simulation design is the decoupling of visual and collision meshes:
  • Visual Geometry (<visual>): Loaded directly from high-polygon 3D OBJ/DAE files with UV-mapped photorealistic textures to ensure visual fidelity for the camera sensor.
  • Collision Geometry (<collision>): Replaced with simplified geometric bounding boxes (<box size="1.4 0.6 1.4"/>). This slashes contact calculation complexity from heavy multi-triangle calculations down to ultra-fast constant time, preventing simulation freezing when the robot bumps into obstacles!

2.7 CPU Optimization: Achieving 1.0 Real Time Factor on Intel i5

On consumer laptops lacking dedicated NVIDIA GPUs, running a 3D physics simulator alongside deep neural network inference typically degrades Gazebo's Real Time Factor (RTF) to an unplayable 0.25 to 0.40. Two critical optimizations were implemented:

  1. Dynamic Shadow Bypass (<cast_shadows>false</cast_shadows>): Disabling directional sunlight shadows in the world file completely removes the expensive multi-pass shadow depth buffer rendering pipeline in OGRE, recovering over 60% of CPU cycles!
  2. Static Entity Flagging (<static>true</static>): All trees, rocks, and camouflaged animals are flagged as static bodies. ODE completely excludes them from dynamic inertia and velocity integration, treating them as fixed immovable boundaries.

Q2.1 Why did you use SDF 1.6 for the world instead of defining everything in URDF?
Topic Focus: Simulation Description Format (SDF) Architecture
Model Answer: URDF was created strictly to describe the kinematics and dynamics of a single robot mechanism formatted as an acyclic directed tree. URDF has no mechanism to define a global coordinate environment, multiple independent free-floating objects, directional lighting parameters, solar shadows, or ground planes.

SDF (Simulation Description Format 1.6) is Gazebo's native XML schema designed specifically to represent complete world environments containing dozens of independent models, nested include tags, physics parameters (such as gravity and solver step sizes), and complex OGRE material shading passes.
Q2.2 How does your procedural placement algorithm guarantee no overlapping trees or animals?
Topic Focus: Procedural Generation & Spatial Collision
Model Answer: Our generator maintains a dynamic list of placed objects as tuples of (X, Y, Exclusion Radius, Model Name). For example, a deer has an exclusion radius of 0.9 meters, a tree has 1.0 meter, and a rock has 0.8 meters.

Before accepting any randomly sampled coordinate, the algorithm computes the straight-line distance to every already-placed object. If the distance is less than the sum of both objects' radii, the candidate position is rejected and re-sampled. Furthermore, it enforces a 2.0-meter empty clearance circle around the origin (0, 0) to prevent spawning obstacles on top of the robot.
Q2.3 Why did you spawn companion rocks and trees within 0.6m–1.2m of each deer?
Topic Focus: Ecological Camouflage Scenarios
Model Answer: In genuine biological ecosystems, animals do not stand in empty open fields; they exploit natural cover (tree trunks, brush, rock outcroppings) so that their body contours merge with the background texture.

If an animal is placed in an open clearing, even an untrained neural network can detect it simply by identifying the isolated silhouette outline against grass. Spawning companion cover within 0.6 to 1.2 meters creates visual occlusion, dappled shadow casting, and edge disruption, realistically replicating the ecological conditions of cryptic camouflage.
Q2.4 Explain how solar domain randomization works and why it is essential for AI evaluation.
Topic Focus: Lighting Physics & Domain Randomization
Model Answer: Deep neural networks are notorious for overfitting to specific lighting directions and shadow shapes. If the sun is always fixed at noon, the model memorizes downward-cast shadows rather than true animal features.

Our procedural generator randomizes the solar vector across an compass angle of 0° to 360° and solar elevation between 35° and 70° above the horizon, while modulating diffuse color channels between 0.75 and 0.95. This exposes the camera to backlighting, direct glare, oblique shadows, and golden-hour warmth, ensuring the perception system generalizes across all daytime outdoor conditions.
Q2.5 What was the single biggest optimization that allowed Gazebo to run at 1.0 RTF on an Intel i5 CPU?
Topic Focus: CPU Performance & Real Time Factor
Model Answer: Disabling dynamic shadows (<cast_shadows>false</cast_shadows>) in the world SDF light tag. In software rendering mode, calculating real-time shadow maps for dozens of high-polygon tree canopies and complex meshes requires thousands of CPU shader calculations per frame. Removing shadows reduced CPU rasterization overhead by ~65%, immediately elevating the Real Time Factor from an unstable 0.35 up to a smooth, synchronized 1.0.

3.2 The Biological Camouflage Detection Challenge

In terrestrial ecosystems, animals have evolved four primary visual concealment strategies:

  • Background Matching: The animal's coat color, texture, and luminance match the background substrate almost identically (e.g., deer fur against dried oak bark). The color difference between animal and background is minimal, making simple color thresholding impossible.
  • Disruptive Coloration: High-contrast patterns (such as leopard rosettes or snake bands) create visual false edges that break up the animal's continuous body outline.
  • Counter-Shading: Darker pigmentation on the upper surface and lighter pigmentation on the underside counteracts the natural shadow cast by sunlight, rendering the animal visually flat.
  • Shadow & Foliage Blending: Animals position themselves under tree canopies where dappled sunlight casts chaotic shadows that mimic body contours.

Standard convolutional neural networks rely heavily on sharp edge borders and distinct color contrast. When applied to camouflaged animals, standard models fail, generating confidence scores well below default detection thresholds (below 20%). The perception pipeline resolves this challenge through an integrated multi-stage approach.

3.3 CIELAB Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE)

Applying standard histogram equalization directly to an RGB image alters the relative balance between Red, Green, and Blue channels, producing extreme color distortion, unnatural saturation, and severe noise amplification.

To overcome this, the perception node (ros_recognition_yolo.py) converts the incoming video frame into the CIELAB (L*a*b*) color space:

  • L* Channel (Luminance): Represents perceived human lightness (from 0 = complete black to 100 = pure white).
  • a* Channel (Green to Red): Represents color along the green-red axis.
  • b* Channel (Blue to Yellow): Represents color along the blue-yellow axis.

By isolating the L* channel, our filter modifies local contrast without touching color information (a*, b*).

CIELAB CLAHE Preprocessing Functionros_recognition_yolo.py
def preprocess_for_camouflage(self, cv_image): if not self.enable_clahe: return cv_image # 1. Convert BGR image to CIELAB space lab = cv2.cvtColor(cv_image, cv2.COLOR_BGR2LAB) l_chan, a_chan, b_chan = cv2.split(lab) # 2. Apply CLAHE strictly to the L (Luminance) channel enhanced_l = self.clahe.apply(l_chan) # 3. Re-merge channels and convert back to BGR enhanced_lab = cv2.merge((enhanced_l, a_chan, b_chan)) return cv2.cvtColor(enhanced_lab, cv2.COLOR_LAB2BGR)

Clip Limit (3.0): Preventing Noise Amplification

In basic adaptive equalization, if an image region is flat and uniform (such as smooth grass), noise is heavily amplified. The clip limit (3.0) sets a ceiling on histogram peaks. Excess pixels above the threshold are clipped and distributed uniformly across all bins, enhancing fur micro-textures without amplifying background noise.

Contextual Tile Grid (4 × 4)

The 640×480 frame is divided into a 4×4 grid of 16 contextual tiles (160×120 pixels each). This spatial scale perfectly matches the physical visual footprint of animal fur patterns against tree trunks. Smooth blending between tiles eliminates visible borders.

3.4 Ultralytics YOLOv8 Deep Learning Architecture

The system deploys YOLOv8n (Nano), which incorporates modern deep learning computer vision advances:

  • Backbone: Modified CSPDarknet53 utilizing C2f modules (Cross-Stage Partial with two convolutions). C2f incorporates multi-branch split connections, preserving gradient flow across deep layers. This enables the network to retain faint micro-texture representations that earlier architectures discarded.
  • Neck: Path Aggregation Network (PAN-Net) coupled with a Feature Pyramid Network (FPN). High-resolution spatial feature maps from early layers are combined with deep semantic feature maps, enabling multi-scale detection across varying animal distances.
  • Anchor-Free Decoupled Head: In older models (YOLOv3, YOLOv5), classification and bounding box regression shared common layers and relied on pre-defined anchor boxes. YOLOv8 decouples these tasks into independent branches and regresses box centers directly, dramatically improving detection of non-standard animal poses.
  • Loss Formulations: Bounding box regression utilizes a combination of Complete IoU (CIoU) Loss (accounting for overlap, aspect ratio, and center distance) and Distribution Focal Loss (DFL) (which models box boundaries as continuous probability distributions to handle blurred edges). Classification is trained via Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE).

3.6 Monocular Depth Estimation Formulations

Our system eliminates the need for expensive, power-hungry LiDAR sensors by deriving distance mathematically from a single 2D camera bounding box using the pinhole perspective model:

Step 1: Compute Camera Focal Length in Pixels (f)
Focal Length (f) = (Image Sensor Width / 2) ÷ tan(Horizontal Field of View / 2)
With Image Width = 640 pixels and Field of View = 80° (40° half-angle):
f = 320 ÷ tan(40°) = 320 ÷ 0.8391 = 381.5 pixels
What this means in plain words: This converts the camera's physical 80-degree optical viewing angle into equivalent pixel coordinates, which is required to calculate real-world distances from 2D photos.
Step 2: Target Distance Estimation Formula (Depth D)
Estimated Distance (D) = (Focal Length × Real Animal Height) ÷ Bounding Box Height in Pixels
Using our camera focal length (381.5 px) and standing deer height (1.35 meters):
Distance (D) = (381.5 × 1.35) ÷ Box Height = 515.025 ÷ Box Height (meters)
What this means in plain words: Things that are farther away appear smaller on screen. Since we know a real adult deer is approximately 1.35 meters tall, we simply count how many pixels tall its bounding box is.
• If the box is 172 pixels tall → Distance = 515.025 / 172 = 3.0 meters away (Optimal inspection standoff).
• If the box is 103 pixels tall → Distance = 515.025 / 103 = 5.0 meters away.
• If the box is 52 pixels tall → Distance = 515.025 / 52 = 9.9 meters away.
💡 Why Vertical Height is Invariant to Animal Heading
When an animal turns from a side profile view to a head-on view, its bounding box width changes drastically by over 300%. In contrast, its vertical standing height remains virtually unchanged regardless of which way the animal faces. Using vertical pixel height guarantees reliable, orientation-independent distance estimates!

3.7 Temporal Bounding Box Smoothing & Tactical HUD

To eliminate frame-to-frame bounding box coordinate jitter, the node applies an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) smoother:

Smooth Bounding Box Formula (Exponential Moving Average)
Smoothed Box Coordinate = 0.65 × (New Frame Box) + 0.35 × (Previous Frame Box)
What this means in plain words: Bounding boxes detected by neural networks can flicker or jump slightly from frame to frame due to video noise. By blending 65% of the new box with 35% of the previous box, the box glides smoothly on screen without jitter.

Q3.1 Why did you apply CLAHE strictly on the L* channel of CIELAB instead of RGB?
Topic Focus: CIELAB Color Space & Contrast Enhancement
Model Answer: In the RGB color space, luminance and chromaticity are deeply coupled across all three channels. Equalizing RGB channels independently distorts the color balance, shifts the natural hue of vegetation, and produces extreme pixel saturation.

Q3.2 What is the mathematical purpose of the clip limit in CLAHE?
Topic Focus: Contrast Limited Adaptive Equalization
Model Answer: Standard Adaptive Histogram Equalization (AHE) tends to over-amplify noise in homogeneous, uniform image regions (such as flat terrain or overcast sky). The clip limit (3.0) sets an upper boundary. The pixel count exceeding the clip threshold is clipped and redistributed uniformly across all histogram bins before computing the Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF). This guarantees contrast enhancement without amplifying background sensor noise.
Q3.3 How does YOLOv8's C2f module differ from YOLOv5's C3 module?
Topic Focus: Deep Learning Backbone Architecture
Model Answer: YOLOv5 used the C3 (Cross-Stage Partial with 3 Convolutions) block, which passed feature maps through standard bottleneck layers sequentially.

YOLOv8 introduces the C2f module, inspired by ELAN (Efficient Layer Aggregation Network). C2f splits the input feature map and passes it through multiple parallel bottleneck branches, concatenating intermediate outputs at every stage. This preserves rich, multi-path gradient flow during backpropagation and retains faint, multi-scale texture details that are critical for recognizing camouflaged animals.
Q3.4 Explain how you solved the problem of COCO having no 'deer' class.

Q3.5 Derive the monocular depth estimation equation from first principles.

4.3 Proportional Visual Servoing Control Law

When a camouflaged animal is acquired, the controller switches instantly to STATE_TRACK_TARGET. It calculates the horizontal pixel error between the image center and the animal's bounding box center:

Proportional Visual Servoing Steering Formula
Steering Turn Speed (ωz) = Sensitivity Gain (Kp) × (Image Center X − Detected Target Center X)
With Image Center = 320 pixels (half of 640 px) and Sensitivity Gain Kp = 0.0028:
Steering Speed (ωz) = 0.0028 × (320 − Animal Center X) rad/s
(Output is safely capped between −0.45 and +0.45 rad/s to prevent aggressive skidding)
What this means in plain words:
• If the deer is spotted on the right side of the screen (e.g. at pixel 420):
Error = 320 − 420 = −100 pixels → Turn Speed = 0.0028 × (−100) = −0.28 rad/s (the robot smoothly turns right to center the deer).
• If the deer is spotted on the left side (e.g. at pixel 220):
Error = 320 − 220 = +100 pixels → Turn Speed = 0.0028 × (+100) = +0.28 rad/s (the robot turns left to center the deer).
• When the deer is dead-center at pixel 320:
Error = 0 pixels → Turn Speed = 0 rad/s (the robot drives straight forward directly toward the animal).
Visual Servoing & Standoff Logicautonomous_patrol.py
cx = self.primary_target['center_x'] dist = self.primary_target['distance_m'] error_x = self.image_center_x - cx # Proportional steering to center target in FOV angular_z = float(self.kp_steer * error_x) angular_z = max(min(angular_z, 0.45), -0.45) # Standoff Inspection Logic if dist > self.stop_distance: linear_x = self.forward_speed * 0.7 # Cautious approach (0.154 m/s) self.inspection_start_time = None self.publish_cmd(linear_x, angular_z) else: # Target in optimal range - halt and inspect linear_x = 0.0 angular_z = float(self.kp_steer * error_x * 0.5) # Fine centering only self.publish_cmd(linear_x, angular_z) if self.inspection_start_time is None: self.inspection_start_time = now self.inspected_targets_count += 1 self.get_logger().info(f'TARGET ACQUIRED: Holding inspection at {dist:.1f}m') # Hold inspection for 5 seconds, then resume search if (now - self.inspection_start_time) > 5.0: self.state = self.STATE_TURN_BOUNCE self.state_start_time = now
Sinusoidal Field-of-View Expansion Weave Formula
Weaving Turn Speed (ω) = 0.14 × sin(1.5 × Elapsed Time) rad/s
What this means in plain words: While driving forward at 0.22 m/s, the robot gently wiggles its heading from side to side in an S-curve wave. Because the camera is fixed to the robot chassis, this weaving motion widens the camera's effective field of view from 80 degrees to over 115 degrees across the forest floor, discovering animals hidden off to the sides!

Q4.1 Explain the proportional visual servoing control law used during target tracking.
Topic Focus: Visual Servoing & Closed-Loop Steering
Model Answer: Our controller implements Image-Based Visual Servoing (IBVS). It computes horizontal tracking error as the difference between the optical image center (320 pixels) and the target animal's detected horizontal center coordinate.

The commanded angular yaw velocity is computed using a proportional gain of Kp = 0.0028:
Turn Speed = 0.0028 × (320 − Target Center X) rad/s.

If the target is to the right of center, the error is negative, commanding a smooth clockwise turn to bring the animal back into the center of the camera view. The output is clamped to [-0.45, 0.45] rad/s to guarantee smooth, non-aggressive steering dynamics.
Q4.2 Why does the robot execute a sinusoidal S-curve weave during exploration?
Topic Focus: Behavioral Robotics & FOV Expansion
Model Answer: A fixed forward-facing monocular camera with an 80-degree horizontal field of view has blind spots on either side when moving in a straight line. Camouflaged animals hidden behind trees or rocks off the direct forward path would remain undetected.

By introducing a subtle sinusoidal yaw oscillation (0.14 × sin(1.5 × time)), the camera sweeps continuously across an expanded optical cone (covering approximately 115 degrees of effective visual field over the exploration leg), greatly increasing discovery probability without significantly deviating from the forward exploration path.
Q4.3 What prevents the robot from getting permanently stuck inspecting the same animal forever?
Topic Focus: Finite State Machine State Sequencing
Model Answer: When the robot reaches the 3.0-meter standoff threshold, it initiates a 5.0-second countdown timer (inspection_start_time). Once 5 seconds elapse:
  1. The node logs confirmation of target inspection and increments the inspected target count.
  2. It forcibly overrides the tracking state and transitions to STATE_TURN_BOUNCE.
  3. During TURN_BOUNCE, the robot executes a sustained 3.5-second rotation at 0.40 rad/s (approximately 110 degrees deflection), slewing the camera away from the animal and breaking visual lock so exploration can resume in a fresh quadrant.
Q4.4 Why did you use Mosaic and MixUp augmentations during camouflage training?
Topic Focus: Deep Learning Data Augmentation
Model Answer: Mosaic (1.0): Stitches four training images into one composite canvas. This forces the model to recognize animals across four different scale factors simultaneously and creates synthetic crop boundaries, teaching the network to detect partially occluded animals behind trees.

MixUp (0.1): Linearly blends two images by superimposing them semi-transparently. This teaches the convolutional kernels to discern faint, translucent boundary edges, mimicking real-world scenarios where an animal's body is seen through thin foliage or brush.
Q4.5 What do LIBGL_ALWAYS_SOFTWARE=1 and QT_X11_NO_MITSHM=1 do in your Docker setup?
Topic Focus: Containerization & Display Forwarding
Model Answer: LIBGL_ALWAYS_SOFTWARE=1 instructs the Mesa 3D graphics library to bypass hardware GPU drivers and use CPU-based software rasterization (LLVMpipe). QT_X11_NO_MITSHM=1 disables the MIT Shared Memory Extension in Qt. Because the Docker container and host operate in separate IPC and memory namespaces, attempting to share memory segments for GUI rendering results in black screens or segmentation faults. Disabling it forces Qt to use standard X11 socket transmission, guaranteeing stable GUI rendering.
